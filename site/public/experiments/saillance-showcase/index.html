Saillance · an alternatives study · data visualization

Did rising income converge the world into a single healthy cluster?

The claim under test: as GDP per capita rose across the 20th century, the world converged toward one healthy cluster, with high life expectancy nearly everywhere.

Below are three views of the same data that differ in what they make visible, not in how they look. Each states what it privileges, what it sacrifices, and the condition under which it would refute the claim. They are ordered from greater to lesser informative power with respect to the question; the reading is left to you.

View 1 places every country at the 2007 endpoint. View 2 tests the convergence mechanism: did the laggards catch up? View 3 is the familiar converging-averages chart, kept last beside its discard record, because it is the one view here that cannot show the dispersion the other two carry.

View 1

Every country in 2007: wealth against health

Privileges the cluster structure and within-continent spread at the 2007 endpoint: every country is one mark, so a continent that holds both its long-lived rich and its short-lived poor cannot hide behind an average. Sacrifices all history, since this is a single snapshot. It would refute the claim if each continent's marks sat tightly together at high life expectancy.

View 2

Who caught up: 1952 start against years gained

Privileges convergence dynamics: a country low in 1952 sitting high on this axis is a laggard that caught up. Sacrifices the absolute 2007 level and the wealth dimension. It would refute catch-up if the cloud were flat or tilted upward to the right, meaning the already long-lived gained as much or more.

View 3

The familiar view: continental averages over time

Privileges the continental comparison a dashboard usually shows, on position, the most precise channel for a quantity. Sacrifices every country-level fact: spread, tails, and which country sits where. It cannot address the single-cluster question at all, because an averaged line looks the same whether or not the countries beneath it agree. That is why it ships last.

Discard record

View 3 is the familiar form, the canonical Gapminder story of rising, converging life expectancy, and it ships last. It was considered as the single deliverable and set aside: its five lines rise and close together, yet that tidiness comes from the continental mean, which averages away the within-continent spread that View 1 carries. The mean is also unweighted, so a continent's line is not what its median person experienced. An animated bubble chart (the Rosling form) was also considered and dropped: it renders the trajectory well but needs motion to read, cannot be inspected as a static document, and still hides the endpoint spread that View 1 makes visible.

Provenance. Gapminder (free data from the World Bank via gapminder.org), CC BY 4.0, taken from the jennybc/gapminder excerpt. 142 countries × 12 years (1952–2007), n = 1704 rows. Units: life expectancy in years; GDP per capita in inflation-adjusted international dollars; population in persons. Transformations: View 1 identity on the 2007 slice; View 2 a per-country delta (2007 minus 1952); View 3 an unweighted mean per continent-year.
